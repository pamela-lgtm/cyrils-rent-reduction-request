Where we are

Three things rent a property. Two of them are already settled.

Location. Weslyn Park at Sunbridge, minutes from Lake Nona and Medical City, K–8 school inside the community. It is a good location and it is not something we can change.

Condition. The home is show-ready. It is a 2023 build with quartz counters, a large island, a two-car garage and three full bathrooms. Nothing about the presentation is holding this back.

Marketing. Already deployed in full: professional photography, MLS syndication to Zillow and the major rental portals, ShowingTime scheduling, and Realtor co-broke — which means cooperating agents can bring their clients to this property rather than the listing depending entirely on renters searching portals on their own.

Price. That leaves price as the only lever we have left, and it is the one lever that works quickly.

Interest and showing activity are not where they need to be now that the market has been tested. Five inquiries and one showing in eleven days, with no application following. That is enough activity to tell us the listing is being seen, and not enough to tell us it is being chosen.

A note on how this set was built

Every comparable above is smaller than your property and every one of them has two full baths and a powder room rather than three full baths. We included them anyway. That makes this analysis harder on your home, not easier: we are measuring it against a set it beats on specification, and the set is asking $2,350.

We could have excluded the smaller units and produced a friendlier-looking number. It would not have been an honest picture of what a renter sees when they open Zillow for this community.

Findings

What the data says

The property is priced in the middle of a stack of near-identical homes

There are six three-bedroom townhomes actively competing in Weslyn Park right now, including yours. Three of them ask $2,350. Two ask $2,400. Yours asks $2,380. To a renter scrolling a portal, these listings are close to interchangeable — same community, same era, same floor plan family, similar photography. When product looks the same, renters sort by price, and $2,380 places the property fourth of six.

The $2,400 band is not moving

2953 Voyager has been asking $2,400 for 21 days. 2948 Voyager sat 39 days at $2,400 before reducing to $2,350 on August 10. The only two leases that closed at $2,400 took 36 and 65 days respectively, and one of those units is back on the market. Meanwhile the recent leases that closed quickly were priced at $2,350 — 6475 Trailblaze went in six days at that number.

The cheapest unit in the community finally moved

6541 Cyrils Drive asked $2,250 and sat 54 days before going under contract on August 4. Two readings of that are both true: price alone does not produce speed, and price is what eventually produced the lease. It is now the benchmark at the bottom of this market.

Supply is being added by owners who could not sell

Two of the comparables above were for-sale listings before they were rentals. 2928 Voyager was listed for sale at $425,000 and withdrawn in May 2026. 2956 Voyager was listed at $435,000 and cancelled in April 2026. Both are now leased as rentals. This is happening across the Lake Nona and St. Cloud corridor: owners who cannot get their price on the sales market are converting to rentals, and every conversion adds a competitor to yours. That supply did not exist in the spring.

Prospects watch the days-on-market counter

Zillow displays how long a listing has been available. Renters read a long-sitting listing as a sign something is wrong with the property and skip it, whether or not that is fair. At day 11 that counter is not yet a problem. At day 30 it becomes one, and by then the reduction has to be larger to overcome it. Acting now is cheaper than acting later.

The July analysis

The recommendation was right on July 27. The market it described no longer exists.

On July 27 we recommended launching at $2,380 and projected 15 to 30 days to lease. Today is day 11 — still inside that window. We are not raising this because the number was wrong. We are raising it because the inventory this property competes against changed after it went live.

The July analysis rested on a specific argument: at $2,380 the property sat just under the $2,400 filter most renters were using, which meant it appeared in searches its two nearest rivals did not. That was accurate at the time. Here is what has happened in the eleven days since the listing went live:

  • August 4 — 6541 Cyrils went under contract at $2,250, after 54 days. In July it had sat 46 days without leasing, which was then the clearest evidence that a low price alone does not produce speed. It has now leased, and it reset the bottom of the market.
  • August 10 — 2948 Voyager reduced from $2,400 to $2,350.
  • August 10 — 6573 Quest listed at $2,350: a 2026 build, brand new, end unit, pond view. This is the strongest new competitor in the community and it is asking $30 less than your property.
  • August 17 — 2963 Voyager listed at $2,400.
  • July 28 — 2953 Voyager returned to the market at $2,400, six days after recording a lease.

Three of the six active listings in this community now ask $2,350. The filter that matters is no longer $2,400 — it is $2,350, and your property sits above it. That is the entire reason for this report.

A rental market can only be measured in arrears. We price on what has closed, because that is the only data that exists; a shift shows up in the numbers weeks after renters have already started behaving differently. The July analysis also noted that Central Florida demand thins noticeably from September. It thinned earlier than that this year, and the section below is our own evidence of it.

The arithmetic

Vacancy is the most expensive expense you have

A reduction feels like a loss because the number goes down. Vacancy does not feel like anything, because nothing happens — and it costs far more.

Reducing to $2,295 costs $1,020 across a full twelve-month lease — less than half of one empty month. Even the deeper step to $2,250 costs $1,560 over the year, still well under a single month of vacancy. And that comparison understates it, because a vacant home still carries the mortgage, taxes, insurance, HOA dues and utilities every month regardless.

The reduction only has to save you three weeks of vacancy to pay for itself. Based on what this comparable set is doing, we expect it to save considerably more than that.

What the reduction is for

We are not trying to fill the property. We are trying to be able to choose.

There is an important distinction here, and it is the reason we recommend reducing meaningfully rather than by twenty or thirty dollars.

A small reduction might eventually produce one applicant. One applicant is not a decision — it is a situation. If that applicant is marginal on credit, on income, or on rental history, you are choosing between a tenant you are not confident in and going back to the market having lost another month.

A meaningful reduction produces a pool. With four or five applicants we can screen properly, verify income, check prior landlords, and place the strongest household in the home rather than the only one that turned up. That is the difference between filling a property and placing a resident, and over a twelve-month lease it matters far more than $85 a month.

At $2,295 your property becomes the least expensive three-bedroom townhome available in Weslyn Park, while remaining the largest, with three full bathrooms and a two-car garage. That is a genuinely strong position, and it is the position that generates a pool.
